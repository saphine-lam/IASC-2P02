1. Flatness

From inside the cave...

A child sees a blurry shadow amidst the darkness of their bedroom. For a child with an active imagination, it may be their downfall when it comes to identifying where the shadows are coming from. To a young child, the world is still an unfamiliar place to them, since they are learning so many new things, everything is possible to them. The word impossible is one that they will not grasp until they are much older. Therefore, it is quite common for children to be afraid of the dark and the monsters that lurk in the shadows. Nick Sousanis’s concept of flatness describes a form of uniformity between our thinking as humans as a standard procedure, almost like products created in a factory, so that we all fit into the same boxes in order to operate at maximum efficiency as parts of society. The concept of flatness can be applied to the lens through which a child sees the world. Guided by their incredible imagination, which then transforms into fear. A child’s interpretation of the shadows they see is clouded and limited by that fear. They are guided by their emotions and feelings since their sense of logic has not fully developed yet. Hence, how flat their thinking is regarding the shadows in the dark. The child may squint or look harder at the shadow, but the shadow remains a looming presence in their bedroom and remains a fuzzy dark block projected onto the wall.

Unable to fall asleep, the child moves the lamp on their bedside table from side to side as an attempt to get a clearer look at the shadow, but to no avail. The child’s thinking regarding the shadow is flattened by their lack of knowledge of what is real and what isn’t in the world. Although everyone’s minds are different and not everyone was afraid of the dark as a child, many of us have had this uniform experience. Our upbnringings are families all vary from each other but some things stay the same. The world was still filled with possibilities of the unknown for all of us as children; none of us came into the world equipped with all the knowledge in the world. We all wondered, imagined and sometimes a little bit too much until subjects of our imaginations became our nightmares or reasons we couldn’t fall asleep at night. Telling a child a story is standardized across the globe and across most cultures; it is a way to teach children knowledge through an activity that most can do. Whether they be fairytales, stories of myth or children’s books, it is impossible for a child to have never heard a single fictional story. Through fiction, all children are introduced to the impossible, the imaginary, but do not have a grasp on what is required for something to be possible. Hence, many children, along with most of us, have had this unified, “flat” experience of being afraid of the dark despite having different upbringings. In this case, the children’s “flat” experience of fear towards the dark is what resembles the worldview of the cave people in Plato’s allegory of the cave.

2. Seeing Double

From outside the cave...

The child’s parent hears all the ruckus their child is making from their bedroom and enters their room to investigate.They open the door, and the light behind them from the hallway illuminates the room and sharpens the edges of the shadow as the door is closer to the object.However, the illumination in clarity is literal in this situation; age and knowledge often illuminate aspects of our lives in the same way. The new knowledge we accumulate through time does not “write over” our knowledge as a kid in any way; it provides us with new perspectives. We as adults no longer believe in monsters that lurk in the dark, but most of us carry the memories of being afraid of the dark when we were younger. That memory allows us to understand how a child may be thinking and the reasoning behind their actions. The parent explain to the child that shadows are casted by objects if they are in the direct path of a light source, that there was nothing to be afraid of, and they could go to sleep without worry. However, it is difficult to explain to a young child that monsters didn’t exist or that shadows were simply objects obstructing the path of light.

Even though age does not give us the superpower to see in the dark and magically identify objects by a blurry dark outline, we are, however, able to understand the logic behind how shadows are cast and use our knowledge to investigate the source of the shadow. From the position of the child’s lamp, the parent comes to the conclusion that the shadow must have come from an object under the bed. They bend down and pull out a lost toy from the other side of the bed.Despite the source of the scary shadow being discovered, the child remains afraid of the dark and the possibility of more scary shadows appearing. Nick Sousanis’s concept of Seeing Double implies that seeing double is a necessary process in order to get a more complete view of the world around us. Being limited to flatness is like seeing only a cube from the top and determining it as a flat shape, compressing the world around us down to one dimension. Brushing off a child’s fear of the dark as a simple irrational fear is just like compressing a cube into a square; the parent would only be able to see one side of the cube. It is easy to brush off a child’s feelings when they seem illogical to us; it seems like they are the ones who can’t understand or sometimes even feel like they are purposely acting out of line. Instead of getting upset at their child for not being asleep despite how late it was already, the parent pauses and reminiscences the times when they were afraid of the dark. The wonder and imagination of a child is their special power, which allows them to learn at a rapid speed and adapt to the world around them. Learning is a process; even as adults, we are constantly learning, finding new solutions and new ways out of all sorts of situations. Children often seek companionship when they are scared; loneliness often amplifies that fear. Remembering their own childhood, the parent leaves the bedroom and returns with their favourite stuffed animal from childhood, telling their child that this was a toy that came to life from their own childhood, who fought monsters and shadows in the dark as they slept. By utilizing a child’s imagination as an advantage rather than an inconvenience, the parent successfully puts the child to sleep with their newly introduced friend. Due to the parents’ effective intervention in the situation, the child’s view on the situation has been broadened. The parents’ intervention in this case is the event that caused the shadows in the cave to appear differently to the people inside the cave. The child is shown perspectives on how the shadows were made, which in terms changes their view on what a shadow is.
